## Une transcription graphématique

Our task[^1] is to find a way of translating the way in which text is delivered on its original medium into a 
machine-interpretable system that can be interpreted by a machine and that promotes learning. Our solutions will 
inevitably be reductive and fundamentally interpretative, since it is impossible 
to render all the variety of handwriting by means of a computer. To define our practices, we will use Dominique 
Stutzmann's definition of:
- *allographic* transcription, a transcription that aims to give access to all the forms of each letter or sign.
- *graphematic* transcription, which preserves the sequence of letters and reduces each form to its meaning in an 
  alphabetic system.
  
In order to propose an accessible transcription system, we rejected the idea of producing allographetic transcriptions, as 
we felt it would be impossible to propose general recommendations for transcriptions of this type for all medieval 
documents from the 10th to 15th centuries. What's more, if we rely solely on the shape of the letter, some allographs
are so similar to each other, such as "u" and "n", "ſ" (long s) and "f", 
that they could be represented by the same letters, if we no longer rely on the meaning of the word, but only on the 
shape of the sign. Pushing the imitation too far would risk making the transcription impossible to complete and unusable.

## Favoring Unicode's public domain

Unicode's public domain is to be preferred to the various private domains (called [Private Use areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_Use_Areas)), even if they have been 
created by initiatives such as [MUFI](https://mufi.info/q.php?p=mufi).
