### Accents

Some manuscripts, notably 12th-century Anglo-Norman manuscripts, may feature accented vowels. These accents indicate a phonetic alteration or hiatus, but noting them can lead to ambiguity. Indeed, how can we distinguish a dotted "i" from a distinguished "i" when the accent may designate palatalization, a hiatus, a tonic vowel or paleographic disambiguation? As these accents are relatively marginal **in medieval documents**, we invite transcribers not to note them. However, the phenomenon may be noted as an exception, depending on the specific problems encountered in a given corpus.

As far as Castilian corpora are concerned, Spanish accentuation appears in the 16th century and becomes normalized from 
the 18th century onwards. We recommend not accentuating texts prior to this period, but transcribing the accentuation 
*when it appears* **in modern and contemporary** manuscript and printed sources.

Due to the automatic calculation of masks around a baseline, it is possible that the accent is too far from the text to be included in the polygon at the time of the transcription. We recommend however transcribing the accents anyways, since technological evolution may lead to a better inclusion of the accents in the line mask. 

**In modern and contemporary documents**, when the accent is not correctly aligned with the letter it should be associated with due to cursive writing, we recommend still placing the accent where it is expected to be found, not where it was effectively traced. 

When the orientation of the accent is uncertain (e.g. a flat stroke above the letter), we recommend following modern accentuation.

### Cedillas

Cedilla e's mark the latin diphthong ae, and are very common in Latin manuscripts from the 10th-13th centuries. 
We recommend transcribing them for medieval French manuscripts, to guarantee encoding consistency.
The combining character to be used is [U+0327] (Combining Cedilla).
