### In modern and contemporaneous documents

<span class="rule">When the accent is not correctly aligned with the letter it should be associated with due to cursive writing, we MUST place the accent where it is expected to be found, not where it was effectively traced.</span>

<span class="rule">When the orientation of the accent is uncertain (e.g. a flat stroke above the letter), we MAY follow modern accentuation.</span>


### In medieval documents

### Cedillas

Cedilla e's mark the latin diphthong ae, and are very common in Latin manuscripts from the 10th-13th centuries. 
We recommend transcribing them for medieval French manuscripts, to guarantee encoding consistency.

<span class="rule">Cedillas MUST BE transcribed with [U+0327] (Combining Cedilla).</span>

#### Accents

Some manuscripts, notably 12th-century Anglo-Norman manuscripts, may feature accented vowels. These accents indicate a phonetic alteration or hiatus, but noting them can lead to ambiguity. Indeed, how can we distinguish a dotted "i" from a distinguished "i" when the accent may designate palatalization, a hiatus, a tonic vowel or paleographic disambiguation? However, the phenomenon may be noted as an exception, depending on the specific problems encountered in a given corpus.

<span class="rule">As these accents are relatively marginal, we MAY transcribe them.</span>

### About polygonization issues

Due to the automatic calculation of masks around a baseline, it is possible that the accent is too far from the text to be included in the polygon at the time of the transcription. We recommend however transcribing the accents anyways, since technological evolution may lead to a better inclusion of the accents in the line mask.
